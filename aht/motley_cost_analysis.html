01What Motley is, and what can't be measured

Motley is not a project in the platform. It is the 3,000-task patch-based contract, delivered through the sheets pipeline. "Patch-based" is a task variety inside that project; its counterpart is "greenfield".

This matters because patch-based cannot be isolated in platform data. Two independent reasons:

  • Hours are project-grain, never task or variety grain. payable_time_revisions.task_type — the one column that could carry a variety — is populated on 0 of 9,977 rows. tsip_tasks.metadata carries a variety marker on 0 of 8,058 tasks. Patch-based and greenfield ran concurrently with the same contributors, so their hours are commingled and unsplittable even in principle.
  • The only patch-based marker predates the hours data. It's a legacy backfill of delivery ZIP filenames onto attempts (metadata->'tmp_deliveries', matching patchbased), covering 3,050 tasks delivered to Meta 2026-02-09 → 2026-06-15. Sheets payable-hours data effectively begins 2026-07. Zero months carry both. The Feb–Jun work was paid off-platform via the ops claim sheet. Greenfield has no marker at all.

So every figure in this document is the sheets pipeline — Motley's delivery vehicle — blended across varieties. The strongest evidence that this is the right basis: ops' own Finance SSOT independently reports $354/task and $957–$1,500 per qualifying task; this analysis gets $464 and $1,190 from completely different inputs.

One knock-on worth knowing: the 2026-08-19 Google DeepMind batch contains 1,900 tasks of which 1,721 were already delivered to Meta between February and June. That is existing inventory monetised a second time at near-zero marginal labour. Never divide a period's labour by a delivery count that includes resold tasks.

02Contract economics: yield is the lever

Revenue is paid per qualifying task, and only 36–54% qualify. Unit cost is therefore cost per completed task ÷ yield — yield is a divisor, which is why it swamps everything else on this page.

The number to put on a wall: 25.8%

Below 25.8% qualifying yield Motley is underwater at contract price. Recent readings: 54% (8/26), 36% (8/27), 39% (8/28). The working range sits only 10–28 points above the cliff, so one bad week takes it within 10 points. Moving yield from 36% to 54% is worth more than eliminating rework entirely. This is the single metric worth a daily alert, and it is not currently reproducible from the database — see what to distrust.

03What a task costs: the loaded stack

Per task reaching audit. audit = done, which is both your instruction and canonical in sheets ground truth.

Two cost buckets are missing entirely

AI / compute is not tracked anywhere. tsip_ai_spend_events has 0 rows, all time. Every model call — rubric generation, prompt generation, SOTA eval, auto-review — is invisible. So $464 is a floor.

Internal ops labour is barely booked — 5 rows, $254 across two months, which cannot be the real figure for SDL and pod-lead time. Both gaps mean the true loaded cost is higher than anything here.

04Rework: half the labour bill

Only 23.7% of tasks reach audit without a redo. 6,472 redo events at roughly $90 each. Rework is simultaneously the cost problem and the cycle-time problem, which is why it is the highest-leverage operational fix.

92% of send-backs are self-fixed (7,071 of 7,716) and only 6% are handed to someone else. Rework returns to the person who caused it — so quality coaching lands on the right people, and there is no hidden population of contributors silently absorbing others' mistakes.

05Contributors: what actually drives cost

Two candidate metrics: AHT (hours per finished task) and first-pass yield (share of a person's tasks needing zero redos). They measure genuinely different things — but only one of them predicts labour cost, and it is not the one I first emphasised.

Correction: your original instinct on AHT was right

I earlier ranked contributors by “cost per clean task” and told you that ranking on AHT would promote the wrong people, citing a 17.7× gap between two contributors. That gap was an artifact of the metric, not a real cost difference. On cost per task fully finished, the same two people are 1.14× apart — $128 versus $146.

The reason is mechanical: 92% of send-backs are self-fixed, so a person's rework is already inside their own payable hours and already priced into their cost per finished task. Measured across the 43 rateable prompt-lane contributors:

So manage AHT for labour cost. First-pass yield still matters — just for different outcomes: cycle time (1.0 day at zero redos, 9.1 days at 10+), reviewer and audit load, and above all qualifying yield, which is the contract-level lever. Track both; don't conflate what each one buys you.

06Cost per task, and the outliers

This is the number you actually asked for: what it costs to get one task all the way finished, however many redos that takes. Not “cost per task that came out clean first time” — that earlier metric was a quality ranking device and I was wrong to lead with it.

Per-task effort is measured from claim-hold time: every claim on that task, by anyone, capped at 12 hours per claim so an abandoned claim can't dominate. Raw claim-hours run about 7.4× payable hours (people hold claims overnight), so they are scaled by 0.1347 to convert to payable-hour equivalents, then priced at the $167.44 blended rate.

The scaling validates itself: it produces a mean of 2.49 payable-hours per task against an independently computed blended AHT of 2.46. Two different routes to the same number, so the per-task allocation is sound.

2,695 tasks with claim data (31 of the 2,726 have none). p25 $77 · median $310 · p75 $583. Standard deviation 17.83 claim-hours against a mean of 18.45 — the spread is as large as the average.

Mean is 34% above median. That gap is the whole story: this is a right-skewed distribution, so quoting an average per-task cost overstates the typical task and understates the problem ones. Use the median for planning and the tail for cost control.

Monotonic: every step up in rate costs more per finished task. $249 → $264 → $320 → $415. The premium bands are modestly faster in the middle (median AHT 1.27h at $190–209 versus 1.62h at $150), but not nearly enough to offset the rate — a 21% speed gain against a 33% rate increase. The premium does not pay for itself on throughput.

The one place it might be defensible is the $210+ band, where median FPY is 39.3% against 23.1% at the base rate. If that quality difference feeds qualifying yield, it could be worth more than the labour premium costs — but that link is unproven, because qualifying yield is not reproducible from the database. Treat the premium bands as unjustified until someone can measure that.

11Gaming signals

I ran five behavioural signals. Two vectors look real; two of the most obvious-looking signals are false positives that would damage trust if you acted on them. Everything below is a signal, not proof — verify before any action affecting pay or standing.

Tier 1 — time booked without engaging the queue

7 people with ≥20 payable hours, ≤10 claims ever, and zero tasks to audit — $81,536 (7.3% of labour). The clearest case is 325 hours and $48,803 against 2 claims and no delivered task. Logging time while never meaningfully opening work is the one pattern here that is hard to explain innocently. Check for a routing or permissions bug first — being unable to claim looks identical from the data.

Tier 2 — seed spam in artifact acquisition

Pay per accepted artifact spans 1,000x — from $1 to $1,050. One contributor submitted 1,008 artifacts for $6,900 ($6.85 each) and another 489 for $700 ($1.43 each), against peers earning $860–$1,050 per accepted artifact. The direct payout is small; the real cost is the review burden, and it matches the SSOT's own note about "large tranches of spam models, 500+ submissions, very low acceptance". Gate on submission volume per accepted artifact, not on volume alone.

Tier 3 — two signals I would not act on

Long days are not abuse. The contributor with 9 days over 14 hours is Jalaj Garg, the top producer — 284 tasks at 1.81h AHT, at or below median. Flagging them would be a serious mistake.

Claim abandonment is a platform problem, not a people problem. 44% of all claims (9,151 of 20,680) close with no submission, and 6 of the 14 highest-abandon people are high producers. It's claim-and-browse behaviour. But it holds 67,436 claim-hours of queue blocking others — fix it with claim TTLs and auto-release, not offboarding.

Your task 1 — "track historical AHTs closely, message contribs"

  • AHT is the right labour-cost metric — your instinct was correct. It correlates +0.970 with cost per finished task. Rank on it and message on it.
  • Track FPY too, but for cycle time and qualifying yield, not for labour cost (it correlates +0.013 with cost). Low FPY hurts you through reviewer load and the qualifying rate, which is where the contract margin lives.
  • Rank outreach by cost, not by rate. The slowest people are the expensive ones: Jared Minor at $1,329/task, then Sophie Xiao, Joy Tokosi and Johnny Tran above $620 against a $249 lane average. The 23 named zero-output people carry $96,660.

  • Give named targets: 1.61h AHT and 30%+ FPY for the prompt lane; 0.35h/shell for decomp. "Improve your AHT" moves nothing.
  • Use per-lane denominators. Decomp taskers must be measured per shell. Measuring them on tasks-to-audit is what produced my own wrong answer — see section 13.
  • Require volume before rating anyone — under 10 tasks, FPY is noise.

Your task 2 — "reduce task cycle time through auto-review"

  • Re-aim it. Auto-review will not cut cycle time directly, because review is already fast and nearly free. Median time in task_review is 0.17h; all automated gates together cost $7/task and clear in under 0.2h. There is nothing left to squeeze there.
  • Point it at first-pass yield instead. Cycle time is set by redo count — 1.0 day at 0 redos, 9.1 days at 10+ — and FPY is 23.7%. Catching defects before submission is what removes redo loops, which are ~half of labour and the cycle-time driver. Same tool, correct target.

  2. Find out why ~61% don't qualify. The SSOT points at GDM linters and a ~20% defect rate with half in-scope workbook errors. If in-scope defects bind, that is the same root cause as the redo loop — one fix, two levers.

15What to distrust

  • Stage cost is an allocation, not a measurement. No task- or stage-level time exists in the platform. Each claim's active window is intersected with the states the task actually occupied and payable hours split pro-rata. Sensitivity on the 12h claim cap: rework is 46.3% of labour at 2h, 52.0% at 12h, 54.2% at 24h. Quote "roughly half", not 52%.
  • Per-task cost is scaled, not directly measured. Claim-hold time is the only per-task signal available; it is capped at 12h/claim and scaled by 0.1347 to match total payable hours. The scaling reproduces the blended AHT to within 1%, but an individual task's figure carries real uncertainty — treat task-level numbers as indicative and contributor-level aggregates as solid.
  • I dropped "cost per clean task" as a headline. It counted only zero-redo tasks as output, which exaggerated differences between people. It survives nowhere in this document; cost per fully-finished task replaces it.
  • Qualifying yield comes from the Finance SSOT, not the database. There is no "qualifying" flag in the schema, so the most important metric here cannot be reproduced or audited from data.
  • Lane assignment is inferred from where each person's claim time went, not their configured role — roles like dec+pr don't reliably indicate output unit. Someone straddling lanes is forced into one.
  • $464 is a floor. AI/compute untracked; internal ops labour barely booked.
  • Seed cost and task counts are period ratios, not cohort-matched. July's seeds are not July's audited tasks.
  • Everything is blended across task varieties — patch-based cannot be separated from greenfield.
  • No figure here is misconduct evidence. Verify before any action affecting pay or standing.
